Best Shingles for Iowa Hail: Class 3 vs. Class 4

What Class 3 and Class 4 really mean, how the shingles scored in hail testing, and what your warranty and insurance do on a hail roof.

Home › Blog › Best Shingles for Iowa Hail

November 2, 2026 • By Drew Heberer, Owner, Black Ridge Contracting

If I was picking a shingle for hail in Iowa, I would look at two things: the UL impact class on the wrapper, and how that exact shingle scored when the Insurance Institute for Business & Home Safety tested it with lab-made hail. I think most people only hear about the first one, and those two numbers don't always agree.

I own Black Ridge Contracting in Ankeny, and we put on roofs across Central Iowa. Here's what I would want to know before spending money on an upgrade.

What Class 3 and Class 4 actually mean

The class comes from a test called UL 2218. A steel ball gets dropped on the shingle twice in the same spot, and then the shingle gets checked for cracks. Class 3 is a 1.75-inch ball dropped from 17 feet. Class 4 is a 2-inch ball dropped from 20 feet, and that's the highest rating there is.

A cosmetic mark doesn't fail the test. UL also says there is "no currently established direct correlation" between how a roof handles hailstones and how it handles steel balls, and that the test "does not evaluate the effect of weathering, temperature, aging or similar effects." Owens Corning says this about its own Class 4 shingles: "The results of this test do not guarantee that the shingle will resist all weather conditions."

So I would treat the class as a starting point. A lot of the regular architectural shingles are rated now too. Owens Corning, CertainTeed, GAF, IKO, Malarkey, Atlas and TAMKO all sell mainstream shingles rated Class 3, which is why this is really a choice between three groups: no stated class, Class 3 and Class 4.

How the shingles scored in hail testing

The Insurance Institute for Business & Home Safety, or IBHS, is a research group supported by insurance companies, so keep in mind they have a stake in fewer roof claims. Their 2026 scorecard rates 27 shingles from 0 to 10. None of them rated Excellent, 23 rated Good and 4 rated Marginal. IBHS says, "If a product rates Marginal or Poor, we do not recommend using it in areas that experience hail."

A Class 3 shingle tied for the top score, and two Class 4 shingles came in Marginal. That's the whole reason I would look at both numbers.

There are limits to this. IBHS says the tests were on "new, unaged products" and that results are "representative of one batch of products from a specific plant." Not everybody in the roofing business likes their method either. A July 2026 trade magazine piece questioned it because Class 3 beat Class 4 in places, and the author is a sales VP at a company that sells asphalt products to roofing manufacturers.

How much hail we actually get here

We went through NOAA's storm report data. From 2016 through 2025, Polk County had hail of 1 inch or larger reported on 27 separate days, and 2 inches or larger on 6 days. The 2-inch days were September 1, 2018, October 1, 2019, July 14, 2020, July 9, 2021, March 5, 2022 and August 19, 2022. NOAA's records include a 3.25-inch stone in Beaverdale on July 9, 2021, and 3-inch hail at WOI-5 in West Des Moines on August 19, 2022.

Those are reports from anywhere in the county, so I can't tell you how often one house gets hit. The 2-inch ball in that Class 4 test is a size we actually get here.

What size hail actually does damage

There isn't one number. It depends a lot on how old the roof is. A 2002 paper from Haag Engineering found 1-inch hail starts damaging older, thinner shingles, and aged laminated shingles were damaged by 1.5-inch ice stones. IBHS says new shingles, rated or not, "should offer good protection for 1.5 in. or smaller hail impacts." The difference shows up at 2 inches.

Age is the part I would pay attention to. A 2025 IBHS study found asphalt shingles "can be ten times more susceptible to damage after exposure to natural weathering and sub-severe impacts, when compared to new asphalt shingles." It was a small sample of six products, and the authors say more testing is needed. But a 15-year-old roof is not the roof that got tested.

I would not count on the warranty for hail

We pulled the standard shingle warranties from Owens Corning, CertainTeed, Malarkey, IKO, Atlas and TAMKO, and every one of them excludes hail. CertainTeed's says "Damage caused by lightning, hailstorm, earthquake, fire, explosion, flood or falling objects." Class 4 doesn't change that. Atlas names its Class 4 shingles in that same warranty, and Owens Corning says on its Class 4 sheet that "Hail damage is specifically excluded from coverage under the terms of its warranty."

The one asphalt exception we found is TAMKO HailGuard, which does have a hail warranty. It covers leaks from hail 3 inches or smaller, you have 90 days after the storm to notify them, the base term is 7 years, and it excludes tear-off. It scored 7.4 with IBHS. We did not confirm who stocks it in Iowa.

Will your insurance give you a discount?

Maybe. The Iowa Insurance Division says Class 4 shingles "may also qualify for insurance discounts." State Farm lists Iowa as a state with roofing discounts and says only certain shingles qualify. Farm Bureau lists an "Impact Resistant UL 3 or 4 Discount," so Class 3 counts there. We could not find a published percentage from any Iowa insurer, so I would ask your agent for the number in writing before you buy anything.

The bigger thing might be how your policy pays on an older roof. The Iowa Insurance Division says many policies now have a roof endorsement that may replace replacement cost coverage with actual cash value. Farm Bureau publishes a sample schedule, effective July 23, 2026 in Iowa, that shows what that looks like on shingles.

Through 20 years the gap is 2 points or less, from 21 through 30 there's no gap at all, and the only big gap is on a roof over 30, where the Class 3 or 4 column stays at 40% and the other one drops to 25% by 35. I would also ask what happens if you want to upgrade after a claim. The Iowa Insurance Division says replacement cost coverage "will pay to repair or replace the roof with materials of similar quality," so I would want your agent to tell you who pays the difference.

What it costs, and what about the cold?

The best number we could find is from IBHS, around 2020. In a survey of seven US markets, material for Good and Excellent impact-rated shingles cost 9% more per square than shingles with no impact rating, and IBHS says prices "varied significantly by market region." That's material only, it's a few years old and it isn't Iowa data. I don't want to throw out a local number that doesn't apply to your house, so we would price both on a written quote.

You'll also hear about SBS, which is a rubber-like polymer blended into the asphalt. Manufacturers say it stays flexible in the cold. We could not find an independent test of that on shingles, and the UL test doesn't cover temperature at all. In 2020 IBHS did find that every impact-rated shingle rated Good or Excellent at 2 inches was polymer-modified. But polymer alone doesn't get it done either, because IKO and TAMKO describe Nordic and StormFighter FLEX 4 as polymer-modified and both rate Marginal.

As for Iowa winters, hail mostly isn't a winter problem here. Of the 2,636 Iowa reports of 1-inch hail or larger from 2016 through 2025, 77% came April through July, and less than 1% came November through February.

To wrap it up

There are three practical steps I would take:

  1. I would start with a shingle rated Good on the IBHS scorecard, in Class 3 or Class 4.
  2. I would call your insurance agent before picking one. Ask for the discount in writing, whether Class 3 counts, and how your policy pays on an older roof.
  3. I would make your roofer write down the exact product name, then check it against the scorecard and check the class on the wrapper.

If you want to see two shingles priced on your roof, we can put both on a written quote.
